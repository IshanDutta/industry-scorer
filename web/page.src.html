How the numbers are built

One industry at a time. Nothing runs until you click an industry. Clicking one you haven't analyzed (or whose results are more than 3 days old) pulls its data, gathers the research and runs the analyst team; clicking one with recent results just opens them. "Run again" deletes everything cached for that industry (its results, research, earnings-call transcripts, every agent report, rule books and the companies' cached statements, plus the shared SPY, macro and world-news caches) and rebuilds all of it from scratch, including the rule books if you had built them.

Every industry in a sector, with one click. Open a sector and choose "Analyze all": every industry in it is analyzed one after another and each gets the same report it gets on its own. What the industries share is researched once for the sector rather than once per industry: one macro planner picks the series for every industry and tags each series with the industries it matters to; indicator analysts explain what is driving each series once and say, for each tagged industry, how much it matters and why; one world-events scout finds the live situations and says which industries each one reaches; each situation's facts (what is happening, the trigger, dated developments, scenarios) are written once, and its transmission into earnings, exposure, winners and losers is written for each industry it reaches, three industries per call so every section stays full length; each country any industry's trade map names gets its US relationship, risks, threats and policy written once, and each industry's analyst writes what it depends on there and who is exposed; each regulator's instruments are chosen once with a top five per industry, each instrument's legal facts and events are written and fact-checked once, and its impact and company examples are written per industry. Judgment is never shared: each industry still gets its own KPI analyst, earnings-call analyst, customers and suppliers, regulation analyst, trade map and supply-chain verdict, macro strategist, regulatory verdict and the full lead strategist with its red team and review. Company-news IDs differ by industry, so in a multi-industry prompt they are shown under aliases and mapped back. A sector of ten industries takes roughly 400 Claude calls instead of roughly 700 one at a time. The run is saved in this browser after every step; if the page closes it carries on when reopened, and if it stops (a usage limit, or Stop) Resume finishes only what's left. The sector overview compares every industry's scores, opportunity call, horizon calls, forward P/E, revisions and positioning, and opens each industry's full report.

Universe. Actively traded stocks in the FMP industry on NYSE, NASDAQ and AMEX above $300M market cap, excluding ETFs and funds. By default this includes foreign companies listed in the US (TSM, ASML, Medtronic); the "US-headquartered only" setting changes that. Share classes are merged and the 50 largest by market cap are kept. A company that stopped trading in this industry under one ticker and now trades under another (a relisting keeps its SEC CIK, e.g. Paramount Skydance PSKY → Skydance Corp SKYD) is found from FMP's own data and kept in the industry even if FMP files the new ticker elsewhere; its price history, statements, transcripts and news before the relisting come from the old ticker, and agents may refer to it by either.

Performance and risk. Total returns from dividend-adjusted closes over calendar windows back from the last SPY close. Cap-weighted is buy-and-hold at start-of-window weights; median is the typical stock; breadth is the share of stocks beating SPY. Risk is measured on a daily cap-weighted industry index over 12 months, with Sharpe against the 3-month Treasury yield.

Fundamentals, valuation and health use trailing-twelve-month figures from quarterly statements, aggregated as sums rather than averages of ratios: P/E is total market cap over total net income, margins are total profit over total revenue. P/E history rebuilds that aggregate at each of the last 20 quarter-ends. Banks, insurers and other financials skip ratios that don't apply to them.

Earnings use each company's last four reported quarters: beat rate is the share of reports where EPS beat the estimate; surprises are medians across all reports.

Scores take every section into account. Each is 0–100 for one investor type and combines 17 pillars. Nine come from the computed metrics: momentum (1M–1Y returns vs SPY, breadth in every window, the median stock), growth, profitability, valuation (P/E, P/E vs its own 5-year history and percentile, P/S, P/B, EV/EBITDA), earnings, financial health, risk (beta, volatility, downside volatility, drawdown, Sharpe, concentration), risk-adjusted return and income. Each metric maps to 0–100 on a fixed reference range, so scores don't depend on which other industries you've looked at. Eight come from the report sections, scored by code from each section's ratings, probabilities and counts, never from free text: the industry cycle and KPIs (cycle position, pricing power, demand and supply drivers, management tone, hot vs cool segments, operating leverage), the macro backdrop (the regime call overall and by horizon, and every indicator read weighted by relevance), world events (each live situation by stance, relevance and status, and the situations' scenario-weighted earnings direction), supply chain and trade (the code-computed system health, the rating, cost pass-through, de-rating risk, country stances, bottlenecks), regulation and legal (the regulatory verdict by stance and intensity, the binding rules in the rule books by severity, policy items, litigation), sentiment and perception, sell-side consensus (buy share and upside to price targets, cap-weighted), and the strategist's outlook (bull vs bear scenario probability, the ranked drivers, the evidence-checked landscape scorecard). Pillars are weighted per investor type (about two-thirds metrics, one-third sections): Income leans on dividends, health and rates; Conservative on low risk, valuation, health and supply-chain and regulatory risk; Growth on growth, earnings, momentum and the industry cycle; Aggressive on momentum, growth and sentiment. A section that isn't written yet has its weight shared out among the others. The lead strategist, which reads everything, can then add at most ±5 for judgment. Open any pillar on the page to see every data point and what it scored. Grades: A 80+, B 65+, C 50+, D 35+, F below.

Foreign companies often report in their home currency (TSMC in Taiwan dollars, Mizuho in yen). Their statements and historical market caps are converted to US dollars at today's FMP exchange rate before aggregating; a company whose currency can't be converted is left out of the fundamentals and noted.

What's saved, and running in the background. There is no server-side database and no built-in list: the industry list is read from FMP when the page opens, and clicking an industry fetches and writes everything for it right then, with every Claude call asking for a fresh answer. A run keeps going at full speed while the page sits in a background tab or behind another window (its waits use a background timer the browser doesn't slow down), and while it runs the page asks the device not to sleep. A web page can't run once it's closed, so each run is also saved in this browser only (IndexedDB) after every finished step: if the page is closed or the tab navigates away mid-run, reopening the page brings the run back and carries on by itself, redoing only the step that was interrupted and whatever came after it. Finished runs reopen as they were, with prices and news as of when they were made; "Run again" fetches and writes everything fresh and replaces the saved copy. The 12 most recent industries are kept; another browser or device starts empty.

Research is gathered by code, not by a model: FMP company news, press releases, market news, SEC M&A filings, analyst ratings and price targets, revenue segments by product and geography from filings, and the latest earnings-call transcript for the 8 largest companies (condensed to their most specific passages); EODHD measured news sentiment and sentiment-scored company news.

Macro indicators aren't a fixed list either. Each run, code lists what the connectors offer that day: the whole Treasury curve, every economic release with a reported actual in the last ~45 days in every country, all commodity, index and US-dollar currency quotes, and EODHD's funding, real-yield and corporate-bond series when that connector has them. A macro planner agent picks the 30–50 series that matter for this industry, groups them by what they mean to it, and says why each was chosen (if it can't run, code picks from the data: the curve, high-impact releases in the countries the companies sell in, and the day's biggest commodity moves). Indicator analysts then take each group and, for every series, write its relevance to this industry and why, the effect (tailwind or headwind), what is driving it (actual vs consensus vs prior, the cause, whether the trend is building or fading), why it matters for revenue, costs or valuation, and what to watch next. Before they write, code gathers the coverage behind every series from the whole run: the CNBC wire, every article the world-events and trade teams pulled, market and company news, earnings-call excerpts, the market news from the day of each economic release and the day after, currency news linked to each currency pair, and topic searches the planner named for each series. Each article is scored against the series (its news phrases in the headline count most), a release in another country doesn't count ("UK CPI" is not US CPI), and the best matches are handed to the analyst along with what the world, trade and regulation teams found. Analysts can also search all of it themselves and read articles in full. For every article they cite, they must quote the sentence that explains the move; code checks the quote is word-for-word in the article and that the article is about that series, and removes the citation otherwise. The quotes are shown under each driver. When nothing relevant exists, the driver says so and explains the move from the data. The macro strategist then makes the regime call from those reads.

World events aren't picked from a fixed list. Each run, the page reads the last 10 days of CNBC's US feed through EODHD (which carries CNBC articles in full), follows the other CNBC desks tagged on those articles, and adds FMP's general market news. It counts the topics that recur, so whatever the world is talking about surfaces on its own. The world-events scout reads that wire, can read articles in full and search further by topic (searches only return articles whose headline or opening actually mention the topic), and groups the news into up to 8 live situations. For each one, code then pulls 30 days of coverage on the scout's own search terms, and a situation analyst writes the deep dive from it.

Trade relations ranks the 10–14 countries with the most operational leverage over the industry (where products are made, where tools and chokepoint inputs come from, where assembly and packaging happen, which end markets matter), with each country's stance and trajectory. Each country's share of the companies' revenue comes from their own geographic segment filings. For every country, code pulls 30 days of trade and policy news, and an agent writes the dependencies (criticality, how long replacing it would take, what breaks if trade freezes), the tickers exposed and through which P&L line, the policy in play and the outlook. Dependencies drawn from industry knowledge rather than a retrieved article are labelled background. The system health score is computed by code from importance × stance × trajectory; the verdict adds bottlenecks and whether pricing power can pass trade-friction costs through.

Analyst team. Specialist agents each write one part: the industry KPI analyst (demand and profitability metrics, unit economics, pricing power, drivers, hot and cool segments, what management is saying), the macro planner, indicator analysts and strategist (described above), the world-events scout and one situation analyst per situation (transmission chain, revenue, cost, demand and regulatory channels, statement impact, exposure map, scenarios, winners and losers), and the regulation, legal and deals analyst. The lead strategist runs last, after every specialist report, every regulatory rule book and the regulatory verdict are in. Code hands it one dossier of all of it (metrics and scores, KPIs, the macro regime with every indicator read, the world-event deep dives and their scenarios, trade relations and the supply-chain verdict, regulation, legal and deals, every rule book and the verdict, sell-side consensus, sentiment and news). It writes the thesis, the ranked drivers across sections, the market's key debates, what the valuation already prices in, where the specialist reports disagree, and what would change the view. Then it builds bull, base and bear scenarios that each say what happens in every section (KPIs and cycle, macro, world events, trade and supply chain, regulation and rule books, earnings and balance sheet, valuation, price action and risk, sentiment, consensus), with probabilities and numbers for EPS change, end P/E and 12-month price move. Code computes the expected return, upside, downside, reward-to-risk and chance of a loss from those numbers, and checks each price move against its own EPS and P/E. The strategist then answers whether this is a real opportunity (yes, conditional or no) with entry triggers, the best tickers to express it and what to avoid; if its answer contradicts its own numbers, the page says so. Each investor type's verdict includes a read of every section. An investment-committee reviewer then checks the draft against the dossier (missed factors, numbers that contradict the data, contradictions, unsupported claims) and its corrections are applied. Each citation chip (N news, P press release, W world news, M filing, E EODHD news, T earnings call, I macro indicator) opens its source; code removes citations that don't match a retrieved item, labels figures without a source as estimates, and only allows tickers from the industry. The lead strategist's judgment moves each score by at most ±5 points on top of the section pillars, scaled down when evidence is thin. All of it runs on your own Claude usage.

Regulatory bodies that matter. The regulation analyst names the 4–8 agencies, commissions, ministries or self-regulatory bodies that actually move this industry's earnings, pricing or volumes, most impactful first, with what each controls and its levers. Each body's rule book is built in two steps: one agent picks its 5 most material binding instruments, ranked by 12-month financial materiality (searching the news as it goes), then each instrument is written up with its seven sections in its own call, so no answer is long enough to be cut off. An answer that is cut short anyway is repaired rather than discarded. The sources are a year of company news, press releases and EODHD regulation and trade news that mention the body, plus searches the agent runs itself. A separate fact-check call then labels every sentence that carries a number or citation: verified (with a verbatim quote linked to the source), corrected, legal background (only for the rule's own legal parameters) or unsupported (removed). Company examples must name a company that appears in the cited article. If the fact-check can't run, numeric claims are held back. If a rule book can't be built from live sources, the page says so rather than showing anything unverified. The overall regulatory verdict synthesizes all the rule books and is fact-checked the same way.

Known biases. Today's top 50 are used for all history (survivorship), and past index weights come from price moves, ignoring buybacks and issuance.
